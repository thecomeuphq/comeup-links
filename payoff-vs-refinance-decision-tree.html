Before You Move Money

Three things worth understanding before you transfer, refinance, or freeze anything.

How do balance transfers really work?

A balance transfer moves debt from a high-APR card to a new card offering 0% APR for a promotional window — usually 12 to 21 months. You pay a one-time transfer fee of 3–5% of the balance moved. The math only works if you divide the balance by the number of promo months and pay that amount every single month. Example: a $3,600 balance on an 18-month promo means $200/month, every month, no exceptions. Miss the deadline and the remaining balance jumps to the card's regular APR — often 25% or higher — which can erase everything you saved.

What is the rebound trap?

The rebound trap is the #1 way refinancing backfires. You transfer or consolidate the balance, the old cards drop to $0, and they suddenly feel like free money again. Within a few months the old cards are charged back up — and now you owe both the transfer and the new spending, at two different interest rates. Refinancing doesn't fix a spending leak; it just gives the leak more room. That's why the decision tree above hard-routes anyone who can't commit to frozen cards into the snowball plan first: kill the habit, then kill the balance.

When does refinancing help — and when does it hurt?

Refinancing helps when three things line up: a high-APR balance big enough that interest is eating your payments, a credit score (roughly 680+) that qualifies you for a real 0% promo or a much lower rate, and the discipline to pay the balance off inside the promo window without adding new charges. It hurts when the balance is small (the 3–5% fee costs more than the interest you'd save), when the payoff would take longer than the promo window, or when freed-up cards become a spending relapse. In those cases, attacking the debt directly — highest APR first, or smallest balance first — is cheaper, simpler, and safer.
