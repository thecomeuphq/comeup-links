Overtime pay questions

The rules behind the numbers, in plain English.

How is overtime pay calculated?

Take the hours you worked beyond 40 in a single workweek and pay them at 1.5 times your regular hourly rate, then add your regular pay for the first 40 hours. Example: at $20/hour with 45 hours worked, that's (40 × $20) + (5 × $30) = $950 gross. The calculator above does exactly this math — and if your job pays a different multiplier, you can change it.

What is time-and-a-half?

Time-and-a-half means 1.5 times your regular hourly rate. Earn $20/hour and your time-and-a-half rate is $30/hour; earn $16/hour and it's $24/hour. It's the minimum overtime rate the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) requires for hours over 40 in a workweek.

When is overtime required under the FLSA?

The Fair Labor Standards Act requires covered employers to pay non-exempt employees at least 1.5× their regular rate for every hour over 40 in a workweek. Federal law does not require overtime just for working nights, weekends, holidays, or more than 8 hours in one day — though some states do. California and Alaska, for example, have daily overtime rules, and California requires double time in some situations.

Is overtime taxed at a higher rate?

No. Overtime pay is taxed as ordinary income, the same as regular pay — there's no separate "overtime tax bracket." A big-overtime paycheck may have more tax withheld because payroll tables treat that check as if you earn that much every pay period. If too much was withheld, you get it back as a refund when you file your tax return.

What is double time?

Double time is pay at 2 times your regular rate — $20/hour becomes $40/hour. Federal law never requires it. It typically comes from an employer's own policy, a union contract, or state law: California, for example, requires double time after 12 hours in a single day. If your job pays double time for some hours, switch on the double-time field in the calculator.

Who doesn't get overtime pay?

Employees classified as exempt under the FLSA — mainly executive, administrative, professional, computer, and outside sales roles that meet the job-duties tests and are paid on a salary basis at or above the federal threshold — aren't entitled to overtime, no matter how many hours they work. Independent contractors aren't covered either. Your job title alone doesn't decide it; your actual duties and how you're paid do.
