Notice Rights Check

Your lender hasn't sent a formal notice. In states with right-to-cure laws, the lender generally must send that notice — and give you the cure window in it — before they can legally take the car. Use this quiet window to call first and negotiate from strength.

  • Right to cure: Many states require the lender to send a written right-to-cure notice before repossession, giving you a set number of days to pay the past-due amount and keep the car.
  • No breach of the peace: Repo agents generally cannot break into a locked garage, open a locked gate, threaten you, or use force to take the vehicle. A tow that crosses that line can be challenged.
  • After a tow: In most states you get a notice explaining how to redeem the vehicle and a window — often around 10 days or more — before it can be sold at auction.

General information only, not legal advice. Repossession and cure laws vary significantly by state and by your loan contract — check your state's rules or a local consumer attorney if a tow is imminent.

Standstill Protocol

Imminent — final warning, or a tow could come tonight
Your play
  1. Secure the vehicle tonight. Keep it in a locked garage or at a location only you control. Agents can take a car from a driveway or street, but they cannot break into a locked garage or breach the peace to get it.
  2. Pull your essentials out now. Documents, tools, medication, car seat — anything you'd need tomorrow. If it does get towed, retrieval takes days.
  3. Call the lender before they call the tow truck. A borrower working a plan is cheaper for them than a repo. Ask for the exact cure amount, the deadline, and a hardship hold — then get the agent's name and a confirmation number.
  4. Get any promise in writing. A verbal "we'll hold off" means nothing at 2 a.m. Ask for the agreement by email or in the lender's app messages.

Redemption Acceleration

Already towed — the car is on the lot and the meter is running
Your play
Every day on the lot adds $40 — waiting a week costs you $280 more.
  • Get your belongings first. In most states you have the right to retrieve personal property from a repossessed vehicle — items not permanently attached to the car. Call the lot, ask what's required (usually ID and the key), and go quickly: some lots charge storage on personal items or dispose of them after a set period.
  • Demand the redemption figure in writing. The lender must tell you exactly what it costs to get the car back. Check every line: past-due, repo fee, storage days. Errors are common — dispute anything that doesn't match your tow date.
  • Know your clock. Most states require notice before the car can be sold, commonly giving you at least ~10 days to redeem. The sale date in that notice is your real deadline — work backwards from it.
  • Reinstatement vs. redemption: Some states and contracts let you reinstate by paying only the past-due amount plus fees (your cure number above). Others require the full remaining balance. Ask the lender which one applies to you — the difference is thousands.

FAQ

Straight Answers

Can they really take my car after one missed payment?+

In many states, yes. Most auto loan contracts say you're in default the moment a payment is missed, and default is what legally allows repossession — it doesn't take three missed payments. In practice, many lenders wait 30–90 days, and states with right-to-cure laws force them to send you a notice and a window to catch up first. But "they usually wait" is not protection. Treat one missed payment as the starting gun, call the lender early, and work your cure number.

What is the "right to cure"?+

It's your legal right — in the states that have it — to fix a default before the lender can repossess or sell the car. The lender must send a written notice stating exactly how much you owe to cure the default and the deadline to pay it. Pay that amount in time and the loan goes back to current, as if the default never happened. Not every state requires it, and the number of days varies (often 10–30), which is why the exact notice from your lender matters more than any estimate — including the one on this page.

What happens to my personal items inside a towed car?+

Your belongings are still yours. In most states you have the right to retrieve personal property from a repossessed vehicle — things like documents, tools, chargers, and child seats that aren't permanently installed in the car. The lender or lot may require ID and an appointment, and some charge a modest retrieval or item-storage fee. Don't wait: lots set their own timelines and unclaimed property can be discarded. Call the lender, get the lot's number, and retrieve your items within the first few days.
