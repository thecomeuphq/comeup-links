Bill Audit Check

Three checks that decide how much of this bill is even real. Every “No” unlocks a money-saving move.

Is the bill itemized?

Move unlocked: Do not pay a cent yet. Call billing and demand an itemized statement with CPT codes for every line. Summary bills hide duplicate charges, wrong quantities, and services that were never rendered — you can't dispute what you can't see.

✓ Good — keep that itemized statement next to your discharge papers for Step 4.

Checked for coding / billing errors?

Move unlocked: Line up the itemized bill against your discharge summary. Hunt for duplicate charges, “upcoded” room or ER levels, cancelled tests that still got billed, and supplies charged at 10–50× retail. Industry audits routinely find errors on hospital bills — every wrong line is leverage.

✓ Good — flag anything left in writing; verbal disputes disappear.

Asked about financial assistance / charity care?

Move unlocked: Ask the billing office for the financial assistance application — nonprofit hospitals are required to have a policy, and many for-profit hospitals have hardship programs too. At many hospitals, applying puts a hold on collections while your application is reviewed. Run Step 3 to see where you land.

✓ Good — ask for the decision in writing and keep the account on hold until it arrives.

Your Defense Plan

Three tiers, worked in order. The highlighted tier is where your numbers say to start.

Tier 1 Charity Care Candidate Start here

If your income qualifies, the hospital writes off most or all of the bill — this beats every payment plan.

  1. Call billing and say: “I'm applying for financial assistance under your charity care policy. Please send the application and place my account on hold.”
  2. Gather proof of income: last 2–3 pay stubs, most recent tax return, and a bank statement.
  3. Submit the full application before the deadline — an incomplete application is the #1 reason people get denied.
  4. Get the decision in writing. If denied, ask for the denial reason and appeal — approvals on appeal are common.
  5. Whatever balance survives drops straight into Tier 3.
Tier 2 Itemized Audit & Settlement Start here

Dispute what's wrong, then offer to settle what's left. Hospitals routinely accept 20–40% reductions rather than chase a balance for years.

  1. Demand the itemized bill with CPT codes (Step 2) and dispute every wrong line in writing.
  2. Ask for the self-pay / prompt-pay discount in the same call — it's often approved on the spot.
  3. Offer a lump-sum settlement from your cash buffer if the discount is deep enough, or carry the reduced balance into Tier 3.

Tier 3 0% Payment Plan Start here

Whatever balance remains gets paid on your terms: a fixed slice of every paycheck, at 0% interest, confirmed in writing. No credit card — a card turns a negotiable 0% hospital balance into 25%+ APR debt and can void assistance eligibility.

Straight answers

How does charity care actually work?

Nonprofit hospitals are required to maintain a financial assistance policy — it's a condition of their tax-exempt status. Discounts are usually based on your household income measured against the federal poverty line: at or below roughly 200% of the line, many hospitals forgive 75–100% of the bill, with sliding discounts up to about 300%. You apply with pay stubs and a tax return, the hospital reviews it, and at many hospitals the account is placed on hold — no collections — while they decide. It can often be applied to bills that are already a few months old, and plenty of for-profit hospitals run hardship programs too. The catch: they rarely advertise it. You have to ask.

Why should I never put a medical bill on a credit card?

Three reasons. Interest: a hospital payment plan is typically 0% — a credit card charges 25%+ APR, so a $2,000 bill can quietly become $2,600+. Leverage: the moment you pay the hospital with a card, the negotiable bill becomes non-negotiable card debt — discounts, disputes, and charity care are off the table because the hospital already got paid. Protection: unpaid medical bills have billing-dispute rights and, in many cases, a grace period before they can hit your credit; credit card debt has none of that. If a billing rep pushes a card or a third-party “medical loan,” that's a sales pitch, not a payment plan.

How do I ask for an itemized bill?

Call the billing department number on your statement and say: “Please send me an itemized statement with the CPT codes for every charge.” You're entitled to it. Then compare each line against your discharge paperwork: check for duplicate charges, services you never received, cancelled tests, and supplies billed at wild markups. Dispute anything wrong in writing (email or certified letter) and keep copies — a written dispute creates a paper trail a phone call doesn't. While a charge is under dispute, ask for the account to be placed on hold so it isn't sent to collections mid-review.
