Your EPP right: the fee-free off-ramp

In many states, payday lenders are required to offer an Extended Payment Plan (EPP) — sometimes called an installment or extended payment option. Instead of rolling the loan over again, the EPP freezes the balance and splits the principal into equal payments across your next several paychecks. No new fees. No new interest. The balance actually goes down every payday until it’s dead.

The catch: you usually have to ask for it before you default — often before your due date — and many lenders only allow one EPP in a set period. They are not required to advertise it at the counter.

Availability and rules vary by state and by lender. Ask your lender directly: “Do you offer an extended payment plan on this loan?” Get the answer — and the plan terms — in writing.

Action Strategy

Three exit doors. Based on your answers, your best first move is highlighted.

TIER 1

In the rollover cycle? Invoke the EPP

Your move

For: storefront or online payday loans in a state with extended payment plan rights, once you’ve rolled over at least once.

  1. Go before your next due date. EPP requests are usually only honored while the loan is current.
  2. Ask for the plan by name — use the script below, in person or in writing.
  3. Get the terms in writing: total owed, payment amounts, payment dates, and confirmation that no new fees will be added.
  4. Stop all new borrowing while on the plan — most EPPs void if you take another loan.
  5. Pay the per-paycheck target above on every payday until the balance hits $0.
TIER 2

App advance or no EPP? Stop the ACH bleed

Your move

For: paycheck advance apps and online lenders that debit your bank account automatically, or states with no EPP law.

  1. Revoke the debit authorization. You have the right to tell your bank to stop a lender from pulling money from your account. Call or visit your bank and say: “I am revoking authorization for [lender] to debit my account.” Do it at least 3 business days before the next scheduled debit.
  2. Tell the lender in writing too — an email or in-app message revoking ACH authorization. Screenshot it. A revocation they ignore is a debit you can dispute as unauthorized.
  3. Add a stop payment as backup. The bank may charge a small fee (often $15–$35) — still cheaper than one more rollover fee.
  4. Run the micro-buyout: pay the per-paycheck target from Step 3 directly to the lender each payday until the principal is dead. Revoking the debit stops the bleeding — it doesn’t erase the debt, so keep paying on your schedule, not theirs.
  5. Delete the app once the balance is $0, and turn off any “auto-advance” or overdraft-style features.
TIER 3

First loan, no rollover yet? Redirect now

Your move

For: you just took the loan (or are about to) and haven’t paid a single rollover fee. This is the cheapest exit that will ever exist.

  • Employer paycheck advance: ask HR or your manager about an advance or earned-wage access. Embarrassing for five minutes — cheaper than months of fees.
  • Call the biller, not the lender: utilities, landlords, phone and card companies will usually set up a payment plan or move a due date if you ask before you miss. They’d rather wait two weeks than chase you for months.
  • Community assistance: dial 2-1-1 for local emergency help — community action agencies, churches, and the Salvation Army often cover a one-time bill or rent gap.
  • Credit union PALs: many credit unions offer Payday Alternative Loans — small-dollar loans with capped rates, built exactly to replace this trap.
  • If you already took the loan: pay the full principal on the first due date, even if it hurts. One tight paycheck beats a year of rollovers.

Straight Answers

What is a rollover?

When payday comes and you can’t repay the full loan, the lender lets you pay just the fee and pushes the loan to your next paycheck. That’s a rollover. The trap: the principal never shrinks. Borrow $300, roll it six times at a $45 fee, and you’ve paid $270 in fees — and you still owe the whole $300.

What is an extended payment plan (EPP)?

A fee-free off-ramp many states require payday lenders to offer. Instead of rolling over, the lender freezes your balance and splits the principal into equal payments across several paychecks — no new fees, no new interest. You typically have to ask before you default, and it’s often limited to once in a set period. Rules vary by state, so ask your lender directly and get the terms in writing.

Can the lender refuse to give me an EPP?

In states with EPP laws, no — if you qualify and ask in time, they have to offer it. Some lenders follow industry best-practice rules that include EPPs even where no law forces them. If a lender refuses or claims they’ve never heard of it, ask for the refusal in writing and file a complaint with your state financial regulator or the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (consumerfinance.gov). A complaint on record changes how lenders treat you.

What happens if I close my bank account?

Closing the account stops the debits, but it doesn’t erase the debt. The lender can still send the balance to collections or sue in small claims court, and a closed account with a negative balance can land you in ChexSystems, making your next bank account hard to open. The cleaner move: revoke the ACH authorization and place a stop payment while the account stays open (Tier 2), or open a new account first, move your money and direct deposit, then close the old one.
