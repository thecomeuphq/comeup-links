    Straight Answers

    Why route the money on payday instead of at the start of the month?

    Because shift-worker money doesn't arrive monthly — it arrives per paycheck, and bills don't wait for the 1st. If you budget by the month, the second paycheck of the month always feels "extra" and gets spent, right before the big bills land. Routing on payday means every deposit gets its orders the moment it arrives, matched to the bills actually due before the next one. No deposit ever sits unassigned long enough to leak.

    What's the separate-accounts trick, and why does it work?

    Keep three homes for money: a bills account (Bucket A lands here, and only bills ever leave it), your main spending account (Buckets C and D), and a separate savings account — ideally at a different bank — for Bucket B. The friction is the point: money you can't see on your debit card balance doesn't get spent at the gas station. One transfer on payday replaces a month of willpower.

    What if the paycheck doesn't cover the bills?

    Then the gauge goes red and the tool tells you the exact shortfall — that's information, not failure. Work it in this order: (1) call the billers before the due date and ask for a due-date move to your other pay cycle — most utilities and lenders will do this free, once; (2) split the period's bills across both paychecks instead of loading them on one; (3) cut Bucket D to zero for this cycle only — never touch Bucket A money to cover spending. If the shortfall shows up every cycle, it's an income-or-bills problem, not a routing problem, and no split will fix it — pick up the hours or renegotiate the bill.
