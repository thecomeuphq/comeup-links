Overtime on 12-hour shifts, in plain English

Why one week pays overtime and the next doesn't, why your differential belongs in your overtime rate, and why a big overtime check gets hit so hard by withholding.

The federal rule: 40 hours in a workweek

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), a covered employee who isn't exempt gets overtime for hours worked over 40 in a workweek, at no less than one and a half times their regular rate of pay.

  • It's weekly, not daily. The federal rule doesn't pay overtime just because a single shift ran past 8 or 12 hours.
  • Your workweek is a fixed 7-day block. It's 168 hours in a row, and it can start on any day and at any hour. It doesn't have to match the calendar week.
  • No averaging. Hours can't be averaged over two or more weeks.
  • Nights, weekends and holidays aren't premium under federal law. The federal rule doesn't require extra pay for night or weekend work, or overtime for holidays as such. Any premium for those comes from your state, your employer or your contract.
  • Not everyone is covered. Some jobs are exempt from overtime, depending on the work you do and how you're paid.

Why one week pays overtime and the next doesn't

Many 12-hour schedules rotate between three shifts one week and four the next. Because each workweek stands on its own, the long week can pay overtime even when the two-week total looks normal.

Made-up example numbers

Week 1: three 12-hour shifts = 36 hours. Under the federal rule, no overtime.

Week 2: four 12-hour shifts = 48 hours. That's 8 hours over 40, so 8 overtime hours.

The two weeks add up to 84 hours. Averaging them out isn't allowed, so week 2 still gets its 8 overtime hours.

If a night shift crosses the start of your workweek, ask payroll which week those hours count in. That can change which week goes over 40.

Your differential belongs in your overtime rate

Overtime is figured on your regular rate, not just your base wage. The regular rate includes all pay for your work except certain payments the law leaves out, and the Department of Labor says shift differential pay must be included.

Made-up example numbers

Base wage $20.00 an hour plus a $2.00 night differential = a $22.00 regular rate.

The federal minimum for an overtime hour at that rate is 1.5 × $22.00 = $33.00, not 1.5 × $20.00 = $30.00.

That $22.00 is your regular rate only if the $2.00 applies to every hour you worked that week. If it covers only some hours, your regular rate is your total straight-time pay for the week, differentials included, divided by your total hours worked.

To see overtime figured on base pay plus your differential, put both into the Shift Paycheck Calculator and compare it with your stub.

Hospitals: the 8 and 80 option

Hospitals and residential care facilities can use a fixed 14-day work period instead of the 40-hour workweek, if they have an agreement or understanding with the employees before the work is done. Under that setup, overtime is due for hours over 8 in a workday and over 80 in the 14-day period. Daily overtime premium paid under this setup can be credited toward the overtime due for hours over 80, so the same hours aren't paid overtime twice.

Your state or contract may pay more

Some states have their own overtime laws. When both state and federal rules apply, you're owed overtime under whichever standard pays more. Employers and union contracts can also pay more than the law requires, like double time or premium holiday pay. Your state labor department and your handbook or contract have the rules that apply to you.

Why an overtime check gets withheld so hard

The IRS lists overtime pay as a supplemental wage, though employers can choose to treat it as regular wages. How much federal income tax comes out depends on how payroll handles it:

  • Paid together, not split out: payroll withholds as if the whole check were one normal paycheck for that pay period. A bigger check gets withheld like a bigger normal check, so the extra hours can lose a bigger share.
  • Split out on the stub or paid separately: payroll can withhold a flat 22% on the supplemental part, or add it to your regular pay and withhold on the total.
  • Either way, Social Security and Medicare taxes still apply to overtime.

Withholding is money taken out ahead of time toward your yearly tax. Your actual federal tax is worked out when you file your return. To see how a heavy week changes what lands in your account, run it through the OT Tax Spike Safeguard.

The federal overtime deduction, 2025 to 2028

A 2025 federal law (P.L. 119-21) lets eligible workers deduct qualified overtime pay from income subject to federal income tax, for tax years 2025 through 2028.

  • What counts: only the premium part of overtime the FLSA requires, like the "half" in time and a half. If your employer pays double time, only the half the federal rule requires counts. Overtime pay the FLSA doesn't require, like a state's daily overtime or a contract premium, doesn't count.
  • Limit: up to $12,500 a year, or $25,000 on a joint return. It phases out when modified adjusted gross income is over $150,000, or $300,000 on a joint return.
  • Who can claim it: you need a valid Social Security number, and married couples must file jointly. You can take it whether you itemize or take the standard deduction.
  • What it doesn't change: overtime is still subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes. It's a federal income tax deduction, and your state may not follow it.
  • Getting it in your checks: on the 2026 Form W-4, enter the qualified overtime you expect for the year (only the premium half counts, as above) on line 1b of the Step 4(b) Deductions Worksheet. If you turn in an updated W-4, your employer must use it, so you can see more in each check instead of waiting for tax time.
  • Paperwork: starting with tax year 2026, employers have to report qualified overtime separately. For 2025, they didn't have to.

  1. Count the hours. Split regular, overtime and double-time hours and see gross pay before taxes with the Overtime Paycheck Calculator.
  2. Estimate take-home. Add your differential and see an estimated net check with the Shift Paycheck Calculator.
  3. Check the withholding hit. Compare a normal week to the heavy week with the OT Tax Spike Safeguard.
  4. Decide if the extra hours are worth it. See what overtime pays per hour after taxes with the Overtime Yield Calculator.
  5. Check the stub. Line up paid hours and differentials against the hours you worked with the Wage Theft Audit. It figures overtime on base pay only, so if you get a differential, the overtime you're owed is higher than it shows.
  6. Look at the whole year. If heavy overtime weeks or side income could leave you short at tax time, plan a set-aside with the Tax Withholding Safeguard, and check your W-4 with the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator.
