Straight answers

What exactly are "disposable earnings"? +

Disposable earnings are what's left of your pay after only the deductions the law requires — federal, state, and local income taxes, plus Social Security and Medicare (FICA).

Everything voluntary stays in the number: 401(k) contributions, health insurance premiums, union dues, parking, charitable giving. That's why disposable earnings are almost always higher than your actual take-home pay — and why a garnishment calculated "from the check you actually get" can still be wrong in the other direction. The cap math in this tool uses the legal definition.

Can they garnish my wages without suing me first? +

For ordinary consumer debt — credit cards, medical bills, personal loans — generally no. A creditor normally has to sue you, win a judgment, and then get a garnishment order. If a consumer garnishment shows up and you were never served with a lawsuit, that's a red flag worth raising with the court immediately — improper service is a common defense.

The big exceptions that don't need a court judgment first: IRS and state tax levies, federal student loans (administrative wage garnishment, after notice and a chance to request a hearing), and child support orders. Those come from the government process, not a lawsuit.

What should I do the day I get the notice? +

1. Don't ignore it. Most garnishment notices include a Claim of Exemption form and a deadline — often 10–30 days depending on your state and the order type. Missing it can waive rights you'd otherwise have.

2. Check the math. Run your numbers here. If the deduction beats the legal cap, you have a concrete, numbers-based objection — the strongest kind.

3. Talk to payroll in writing. Payroll must follow the order, but they also must follow the law. A written correction memo (Tier 2 above) creates a paper trail a verbal chat doesn't.

4. Protect the essentials. Freeze non-essential spending until you know your guaranteed net, and call any lender whose payment will bounce — a call before a missed payment is worth ten after.

Educational information, not legal advice. This tool applies the federal rules under Title III of the Consumer Credit Protection Act, IRS levy exemption formulas (Publication 1494), federal student-loan administrative garnishment limits, and common child-support caps, plus stronger state protections where they're well established. State rules change, individual orders vary, and figures here are estimates for planning — not a substitute for a consumer-law attorney or your court's clerk.
