Straight Answers

What is the 30% rule, and where does it come from?

The long-standing rule of thumb says housing should cost no more than 30% of your income — above that, you're officially "cost-burdened." This engine runs the rule harder than the banks do: it measures against your guaranteed take-home pay, not your gross and not your best month. Under 30% of base net, a lease survives slow weeks, cut shifts, and a busted transmission without a missed payment.

Why judge rent on base pay instead of pay with overtime?

Because overtime is the first thing to disappear. Production slows, a line goes down, a new hire takes the extra shifts — and the OT you were counting on is gone, but the rent is still due on the 1st. If base pay alone covers the lease, OT becomes what it should be: fuel for debt payoff, savings, and getting ahead — not a load-bearing wall in your housing.

How does the first + last + deposit math actually work?

Many landlords collect three chunks at signing: the first month's rent (paying for the month you're about to live there), the last month's rent (held and applied to your final month), and a security deposit (held against damage, refundable if you leave the place as you found it). On a $1,150 lease that's $1,150 × 3 = $3,450 due before you get the keys — plus moving costs. This engine adds a buffer on top so signing day doesn't drain you to zero. Not every landlord charges all three; set any line to $0 if yours doesn't.
