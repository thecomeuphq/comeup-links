Straight answers

        

          Is short pay actually this common?
          
Yes — and it usually isn't one big theft, it's small leaks. The U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division recovers hundreds of millions of dollars in back wages every year, and the most common causes are boring: unpaid pre- and post-shift work, automatic meal-break deductions for breaks never taken, time-clock rounding that only ever rounds down, and differentials that never got applied. Warehouse, healthcare, food service, and manufacturing shifts get hit hardest because the work starts before the clock does.

        

        

          What if HR or payroll ignores my dispute?
          
Escalate in writing, in order. First, resend the dispute with your time records attached and ask for a written response with a date. If nothing moves within one or two pay cycles, you can file a wage claim with your state labor department (usually free, often online) or a complaint with the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division at 1-866-487-9243. Keep every paystub, schedule, and message — claims are won on records, and there's generally a two-to-three-year window to recover back wages under federal law.

        

        

          How do I log my time without getting in trouble?
          
Keep it personal and boring. A notes app entry per shift — clock-in, clock-out, breaks taken or skipped, prep time before your shift — timestamped and kept on your own phone or notebook, not company systems. Photographing your posted schedule each week takes ten seconds. Tracking your own hours is legal, it's not confrontation, and most payroll departments fix discrepancies faster when a worker shows up with dates and numbers instead of a complaint.

        

        
This tool is an educational estimator, not legal advice and not an official wage claim. It calculates from the numbers you enter and standard assumptions (overtime after 40 hours in a workweek, prep and worked-through breaks valued at your base rate). Your actual rights depend on federal, state, and local law and your employer's policies. If you believe wages were withheld, your state labor department or an employment attorney can review your specific situation.
