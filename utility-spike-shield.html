STEP 4

Defense Strategy

Three tiers. Your status and spike size decide which one is your move right now — the others stay loaded for when the season turns.

Tier 1 — Shutoff Risk

Shutoff Freeze Protocol

Your move

If a final notice is in hand, the goal for the next 24 hours is simple: get a human on the phone and get a hold placed before the shutoff date. Utilities would rather collect slowly than not at all — but you have to ask, out loud, before the truck rolls.

  1. Call before the date on the letter — use the number printed on the notice, not a search result.
  2. Read the script below, word for word if you need to. Ask for a 30-day disconnection hold plus a payment plan.
  3. Get the confirmation number and the rep's name. Ask for the agreement in writing or by email.
  4. Apply for LIHEAP the same day (below) — a crisis grant can pay the past-due balance directly and strengthens your hold.
Copy-paste hardship script — say it or send it
  • LIHEAP (Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program): federal money, run by your state, that pays heating and cooling bills for income-qualifying households. Its crisis component exists specifically to stop shutoffs and restore service. Apply through your local Community Action Agency, at energyassistance.us, or by dialing 2-1-1.
  • Medical-necessity protection: in most states, a signed form from a doctor or clinic — stating that someone in the home relies on powered medical equipment, or that shutoff would seriously harm their health — forces the utility to delay disconnection, commonly 30 days at a time and renewable. Ask the rep: "What is your medical certificate process?"
  • Winter/summer moratoriums: many states ban shutoffs entirely during extreme-temperature months. Ask whether your state has one in effect right now.
Tier 2 — High Volatility

Equal Payment Smoothing Plan

Your move

When your peak jumps more than 75% over baseline, willpower budgeting loses to weather. Most utilities will flatten the bill for you — free — through budget billing (also called levelized, averaged, or equal-pay billing). Enrollment takes one call:

  1. Ask by name: "I want to enroll in budget billing / levelized billing." It's offered by nearly every major electric and gas utility.
  2. They average your last 12 months of usage and bill you that flat amount every month — for your numbers, about $0/mo.
  3. Know the true-up: once a year (or monthly, rolling) they compare what you paid vs. what you actually used and settle the difference. Slightly overpaid = credit. Slightly under = a modest catch-up added on.
  4. Eligibility is usually light: an account in decent standing, sometimes 12 months of service history at the address. Past-due? Ask about enrolling with a payment plan — many utilities allow both at once.
  5. Keep the sinking set-aside anyway. Budget billing smooths the bill; your own $0/paycheck fund covers the true-up and any rate hikes. Belt and suspenders.
Tier 3 — Fortified

Off-Peak Sinking Set-Aside

Your move

No crisis, manageable spike — this is where you get ahead of next season while bills are cheap. The move is a small automatic transfer, on payday, during the months your bill runs below the yearly average:

  • Automate $0 per paycheck during off-peak months into a separate savings pocket labeled "Utilities." Most households land in the $15–$30 range — yours is sized exactly to your spike.
  • By the time peak season opens, you'll have banked ≈ $0 — enough to cover every month the real bill runs over the flat line.
  • Peak months: pay the bill from the fund, keep setting aside the flat $0, and the "spike" becomes a bookkeeping entry, not an emergency.
  • Never let the fund do double duty. The month it quietly becomes grocery money is the month August wins.

Straight answers

How does levelized (budget) billing actually work?

Your utility adds up your last 12 months of bills, divides by 12, and charges you that average every month. You still use the same power — you just stop paying for August in August.

Periodically — monthly on a rolling basis with some utilities, once a year with others — they reconcile: if you used more than you paid for, the difference is added gradually or settled in a "true-up" month; if you used less, you get a credit. You stay responsible for your actual usage, so the per-paycheck sinking set-aside in Tier 3 is what keeps a true-up from stinging.

What does LIHEAP cover, and who qualifies?

LIHEAP is a federal program, administered by states, that helps income-qualifying households with energy costs. Depending on your state it can cover:

  • Heating and cooling bills — a benefit paid directly to your utility.
  • Crisis assistance — emergency help to stop a shutoff, restore service, or repair/replace a broken furnace or AC.
  • Weatherization — sealing and insulation work that lowers every future bill.

Eligibility is income-based (often up to 150% of the federal poverty line, varies by state), and households with seniors, young children, or disabled members are frequently prioritized. Apply via your local Community Action Agency, energyassistance.us, or dial 2-1-1. Bring ID, proof of income, and a recent utility bill.

I'm on night shift. How do I cut the day-sleep energy bill?

Sleeping through the hottest hours means your AC works its hardest shift while you're unconscious. Target the sleep room, not the whole home:

  • Zone it: close vents and doors to unused rooms; cool the bedroom hard and let the rest of the home drift a few degrees warmer.
  • Blackout curtains on sun-facing windows — they block heat gain and help you sleep. Cheap, immediate payoff.
  • Pre-cool before your shift ends: drop the thermostat while morning air is cooler, then let it coast up slowly during the day.
  • A fan in the sleep room lets you set the thermostat 3–4° higher and feel the same — fans cool people, not rooms, so run it only while you're in there.
  • Check for a time-of-use rate: some utilities charge far less overnight. If yours does, run laundry, dishes, and charging on your "day" — the hours before your shift.
