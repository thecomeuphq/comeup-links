Before you sign anything

How does the balance transfer fee actually work?

The fee is a percentage of everything you move, charged up front and usually rolled into the new loan. Move $8,000 at a 3% fee and you owe $8,240 before a cent of interest. That fee is only worth paying if the interest you avoid beats it — which is exactly what the payback threshold above measures: the month where your savings finally cover the fees. If you'd pay the debt off before that month anyway, the transfer loses money.

What's the extended-term trap?

A longer term shrinks the monthly payment, which feels like winning — but every extra month is another month of interest. A $8,000 balance at 12% over 60 months costs roughly $2,680 in interest; over 36 months it's about $1,560. The payment dropped, the total bill grew. If the new term runs longer than your current payoff timeline, this simulator flags it yellow even when the rate looks better.

Why is reusing the old cards such a big deal?

Consolidation doesn't remove debt — it moves it. The moment the old cards show a $0 balance, they look like free money again. Swipe them back up and you now owe the consolidation loan AND the rebuilt card balances — the classic rebound that leaves people worse off than before they "fixed" it. That's why a yes answer forces a red verdict here: freeze the cards in a block of ice, cut them, or close them first, then consolidate.

Estimates only — this simulator assumes fixed APRs, on-time payments, and that each current debt is paid at its stated minimum until gone.
