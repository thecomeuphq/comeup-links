      Straight answers

      What LTV do lenders actually want to see?
      Most banks and credit unions cap auto refinances around 120–125% LTV — they'll lend a bit more than the car is worth, but not much more. The best rates usually require LTV at or under 100%, meaning you owe no more than the car is worth. That's why the principal strike targets 100%: cross that line and you stop negotiating from weakness. Some subprime lenders advertise up to 150%, but the rate they charge for it usually makes the trap worse, not better.
      Why should I never ignore the lender's calls?
      Because silence is the one thing that turns a fixable problem into a repossession. Lenders have hardship departments with real tools — deferrals, payment plans, due-date changes — but they only offer them to borrowers who call before the account goes seriously delinquent. Every unanswered call gets logged as a borrower who's checked out, and in most states a lender can repossess after a default without going to court first. One uncomfortable 15-minute call is cheaper than a repo fee, a deficiency balance, and seven years of credit damage.
      Voluntary surrender vs. repossession — is one better?
      Slightly, and only slightly. With a voluntary surrender you hand the car back on your terms: no tow-truck fees added to your balance, no middle-of-the-night scene at work, and the account is sometimes reported as "voluntary surrender" rather than "repossession" — lenders reading your file later may view it a touch more favorably. But be clear-eyed: both are defaults. Both crater your credit for up to seven years, and in both cases you can still owe the deficiency balance — the gap between what you owed and what the car sells for at auction, plus fees. Surrender is the last resort after deferral, refinancing, and a private sale (with a gap loan or cash to cover the difference) have all been ruled out.
