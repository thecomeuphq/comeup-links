Side-Hustle Tax Basics

What is self-employment tax?

When you work a W-2 job, your employer quietly pays half of your Social Security and Medicare taxes. When you're self-employed — DoorDash, Uber, freelancing, selling digital products — you're both the worker and the employer, so you pay both halves: 15.3% total (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare).

It's charged on 92.35% of your net profit (the IRS gives you a small break because employers get one too), and you can deduct half of it from your income tax. It applies once your net self-employment earnings pass $400 for the year — even if you owe no income tax at all.

Why set money aside for quarterly taxes?

No employer is withholding tax from your payouts, so the IRS expects you to pay as you go — in four estimated installments (roughly mid-April, mid-June, mid-September, and mid-January). Wait until April to pay it all and you can get hit with an underpayment penalty on top of a painful lump-sum bill.

The set-aside rule above does the hard part for you: skim the percentage off every payout into a separate account, and each quarterly payment is already sitting there when the deadline lands.

What counts as a deductible business expense?

Expenses that are ordinary and necessary for your hustle: business mileage on your car (most drivers use the IRS standard mileage rate instead of tracking gas and repairs), platform and payment-processing fees, supplies and inventory, packaging and shipping, software and app subscriptions you use for work, and the business share of your phone bill.

Every deductible dollar reduces your net profit — which lowers your self-employment tax and your income tax. Keep receipts and a mileage log as you go; expenses you can't document are expenses you can't claim.
