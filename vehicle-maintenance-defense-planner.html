Field Questions

Why does a breakdown hit shift workers harder than anyone else?

Office workers can work from a couch with a dead car. A shift worker can't. No ride means no shift, no shift means no check — and most warehouse and plant jobs run on strict attendance point systems, so one breakdown can cost the job itself, not just a day's pay.

That turns a $600 repair into a five-figure income problem. For anyone whose paycheck depends on physically showing up, the vehicle isn't transportation — it's income equipment, and it has to be defended like it.

Repair it or replace it — how does the math actually work?

Compare the repair against one year of payments on the replacement. A $1,800 transmission job feels brutal — but a $350/month car note is $4,200 a year, plus higher insurance, plus a down payment gone. If the repair buys you another year or more of driving, it usually wins by thousands.

Replace when repairs stack past roughly 50% of the vehicle's value in a single year, when the frame or structure is compromised, or when it leaves you stranded repeatedly — reliability is the whole point. Run the predicted total from Step 2 against your vehicle's value: that's your repair-vs-replace line.

What's the $500 buffer rule?

Before aggressive extra debt payments, stack $500 in a dedicated repair buffer. $500 covers the most common job-killers — a battery, a starter, a set of brake pads, a tow plus a minor fix — in cash, same day, no credit card.

Without it, every breakdown goes on a card at 25%+ interest, and the "extra debt payment" you just made comes right back with interest attached. Buffer first, then attack debt, then grow the buffer into a full maintenance fund using the per-paycheck set-aside from this plan.
